Himalaya Trek Planner

Plan a trekking company in a Himalayan valley. Read the maps, weigh the trade-offs, commit to a plan — then run your first season and live with the consequences.

Your problem: Tourists pay to see high, dramatic places — the sacred peak, the glacial lake, the high pass. But a base camp needs to be lower (safe to sleep, time to acclimatise), on flat ground, near water and access, away from hazards and sacred land — and not too far from the attraction, or clients can't make the trek. Find the balance. The maps hold the evidence; you have to infer the consequences.

What will you sell?

Pick the high attraction clients pay to reach. Higher = more dramatic = more they'll pay, but harder to reach safely.
Above 3,000m, clients shouldn't gain more than about 500m of sleeping height per day. How much height is there between your base and what you're selling? How far across the map? And look at what the route would cross — are there contour lines so tightly packed they form a near-vertical wall? Trekking parties can't cross those.

Religion & sacred places

The valley’s people are mostly Tibetan Buddhists (the lower farms blend Buddhist and Hindu practice). Prayer flags and stone chortens mark the trails; trekkers walk to the left of chortens and prayer walls, clockwise around them.

Two places are sacred and off-limits to commercial development:

• Tashi Gompa at grid reference F4. This Buddhist monastery (a gompa) was founded by a revered lama in the 19th century. The land around it is consecrated. Building a base, camp or lodge here is refused by the council and offends the community deeply. Visiting the monastery itself — quietly, modestly dressed, hat off — is welcomed.

• Khumla Ri at grid reference M9. This peak is locally known as the dwelling of a protector deity. The summit may never be climbed; the viewpoint at its base is the only acceptable goal. You can sell the view; you cannot sell the climb.

The local lama blesses the trekking season at a small ceremony each autumn. Companies that attend in person and donate to the monastery upkeep are viewed more warmly than those that don’t.

Solu La (the high pass at N10), by contrast, is not sacred — it is a working trade route and crossings are unrestricted, weather permitting.

Communities & the local economy

About 3,000 people live in the valley year-round. Sherpa families dominate the upper valley around Sherpa lodge village (G6) and have worked in trekking and mountaineering for over a century — most adult men in this village have summit experience.

Lower down, the Farming village (D2) is a mixed Sherpa–Rai community of subsistence farmers who terrace-cultivate barley, potato and buckwheat. They sell surplus to passing trekkers but trekking is not their main income.

Where tourist money lands matters. A company that bases near a village hires local guides and porters, fills local teahouses, and the income spreads. A camp set up in the wilderness, far from any settlement, brings little to the people who live here — the council notices.

Yak husbandry remains important above 3,500m. Yak-driver families graze their animals on the alpine meadows from June to September; trekking parties should give yaks the uphill side of the trail when they pass.

Yearly tourist revenue across the valley is estimated at USD 4 million, of which only about 30% is thought to reach local pockets. Companies that hire local, use local food and accommodation, and avoid undercutting community rates are welcomed; those that fly in their own staff and supplies less so.

A traditional craft worth knowing about is the weaving of wool radi mats, which makes for an authentic gift for clients.

Calendar & behaviour

Two festivals fall within typical trekking windows:

• Dashain & Tihar (Oct–Nov): Nepal’s biggest Hindu festivals, observed in the lower villages. Many shops and bus services pause for several days. Trekkers can experience the lights, music and family feasts — a real cultural drawcard.

• Mani Rimdu (Oct or Nov): a three-day Buddhist festival held at Tashi Gompa with masked dances. Trekkers may attend respectfully. Do not photograph during the rites without permission.

Tibetan New Year (Losar) falls in February or March and is celebrated more quietly at home.

A famous British mountaineer attempted Khumla Ri in 1953 and was turned back not by the mountain but by the community; her account is in the Alpine Journal.

Trekkers are expected to dress modestly (shoulders and knees covered in villages), remove shoes before entering homes and monasteries, and not point feet at altars. Photography is welcome outdoors but ask before photographing people, especially monks.

Local guides earn roughly USD 25 per day; porters carry up to 30kg and earn around USD 15 per day. Tipping at trek end is customary, around 10% of the trek price.
