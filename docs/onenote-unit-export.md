## Reading The Bone Sparrow (course welcome page)

Welcome to the Text Analysis course focusing on *The Bone Sparrow* by Zana Fraillon. Over the next 9 weeks, we will take a deep dive into this powerful novel, exploring its big ideas, characters, language and symbols.

**Expectations:** bring your copy of the novel to every lesson; participate in discussions; complete weekly readings and annotate as you go; complete learning activities that develop thinking, writing and speaking; engage with ideas and characters; prepare for assessments and seek feedback.

**Part I (weeks 1–4):** background, characters, setting. Read chapters 1–17, annotating key ideas, symbolism and descriptive writing. Class discussions and character monologues explore how writers give characters a "voice". **Assessment: oral presentation** — give voice to a character from the story (motivations, feelings, experiences), presented in an engaging and insightful way.

**Part II:** learn to write analytic sentences about characters, ideas and language. Finish the novel; discuss how structure shapes the audience's thinking. Practise writing through journal entries, character analysis and other activities. **Assessment: text response essay** — explore how key ideas are developed through character, language and symbol, with close analysis and evidence.

## Lesson 01 — Pre-reading activities

**Learning intention:** To begin thinking about refugees and those seeking asylum in Australia.
**Success criteria:** I have reflected on what I would take with me; I have identified simple, compound and run-on sentences.

**Engage — What would you take with you?** (Think, table, share.) Scenario: civil war in South-Eastern Australia; boats to New Zealand; you can pack only a small backpack. What will you take? 1 min silent think, 4 min table share, 5 min class share.

**Develop:** Watch *What they took with them* (UNHCR). Complete the sentences: "If I had to escape with only a backpack, I would take…"; "Some other good suggestions I heard were…".

**Apply — Vocabulary building:** sort words into Unknown / Acquainted / Established: Immigration, refugee, asylum seeker, persecution, inmate, incarceration, bureaucracy, off-shore detention, trauma, identification, dislocation, disheartened, dispossessed, bystander, grief, loss, friendship.

**Group task — Types of sentences:** each group receives an envelope of fifteen sentences about asylum seekers in Australia. Read for content first; then sort by construction (commas, full stops, conjunctions) into three piles. *(Teacher's version: students help define each category; explicitly teach run-on sentences and how to fix the error. Simple = one clause; compound = two clauses joined by conjunction; run-on = two clauses missing full stop/conjunction.)*

**Review/reflect:** differences between piles; new learning; difference between simple and compound sentences; two ways to fix a run-on.

**You will need:** Asylum Seeker concept attainment cards (1 set per group); UNHCR video.

## Lesson 02 — Sentence types

**Learning intention:** improve understanding of sentence types; learn to avoid run-on sentences.

**Definitions to record:** Conjunction (FANBOYS — for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so); Simple sentence (single clause); Compound sentence (two clauses joined by conjunction); Run-on sentence (two clauses, missing full stop or conjunction).

**Task 1:** copy and underline the conjunctions:
- We are going to read Bone Sparrow, but we are not expected to have read it yet.
- I need to bring the book to class, but we might not read every lesson.
- I might find the language easy or I might find it challenging.
- We will read a section at a time and we will discuss the ideas in the story.

**Task 2:** correct by adding conjunction or full stop:
- The cover contains an image of a boy sitting alone, so the story is probably about loneliness.
- There is barbed wire that turns into birds, as these are symbols of prisons and freedom.
- The cover is blue, and this can represent the ocean and also sadness.

**Group task:** does a full stop and a conjunction convey the same meaning? When choose which?

## Lesson 03 — Beginning the book

**Learning intention:** identify main ideas in the story; improve reading fluency.
**Success criteria:** read chapters 1–2; annotate with symbols.

**Engage — Reading with a pencil:** cover analysis recap — barbed wire turning into birds (imprisonment/freedom); boy alone on blue (loneliness/sadness) but blurb mentions friendship; boy looks skyward + blurb mentions imagination (expect imaginative descriptions).

**Develop — Things we don't understand:** authors create tension by hinting at ideas we don't understand yet ("Who is Queeny!?"). Hold those questions; the author wants the discomfort.

**Apply:** while teacher reads chapter 1, pairs write down questions of this kind; draw idea symbols in margins next to relevant paragraphs.

**Explore/extend:** flag sensory descriptions and figurative language (simile, metaphor, personification) — what tone or emotion do they add?

Classroom posters exist for each big idea.

## Lesson 04 — Finding evidence

**Learning intention:** understand "evidence" when reading a text; find evidence in a text.

**Shared definition:** *Evidence — a sentence or a phrase from a text that proves or supports an idea.*

**How to find evidence:** 1) know what you need to find; 2) think where in the story it appears; 3) skim and scan first sentences of paragraphs; 4) re-read to find the sentence/phrase; 5) copy word-for-word; 6) conventions — quotation marks, page number in brackets, correct capitals/punctuation.

**Apply:** complete `04w - Chapter 1-2 evidence` — find quotes for: when Maá last ate a proper meal; that Eli lives in a detention centre; food shortages; what Food Tent food looks like; hearing his father in the shell; pictures in the mould; Harvey caring about the children; magazine photos of the sea; putting his head under water in the pool. Example given: "This morning I found a shell washed up right along with those animals…" (p. 2).

**Review:** fist-to-five on "I can describe what evidence means" and "I was able to find my own evidence".

## Lesson 06.1 — Finding evidence / 06.2 — Embedding evidence

**06.1 intention:** find my own evidence to support an idea; collect at least four quotes from chapter 5.
Key teaching: quotation marks mean "this is exactly what the character said" (in a story) vs "this is exactly what the book said" (when writing about a story).

**06.2 intention:** embed a quote into an explanation. Engage: the word *bed* inside *embed* — embedded means "placed inside" something else. PowerPoint: Lesson 06 — embedding quotes.

**06w — Embedding evidence (Chapter 5)** — worked example:
Q: According to Eli, what makes Beaver so mean?
Interpretation: Eli believes Beaver hates most of the refugees because he almost lost his life when a man attacked and maimed him.
Evidence: "almost got killed when a man in here turned crazy and grabbed a hammer." (p. 31); "Beaver ended up losing his eye because of it…" (p. 31); "his eyelid sewn down flat over where his eye used to be." (p. 31)

Student questions: 1) How does Subhi react when he is caught by Beaver? (p. 33) 2) How does Subhi feel about Queeny in this chapter? (p. 34) 3) What are the "Listen Now" stories Maá tells depicting? (p. 36) 4) Why are the stories they tell each other, and storytelling generally, so important? (p. 37). Then: embed a quotation in one answer.

Self-assessment tool: `06a - Finding evidence`.

## Lesson 07 — Character Voice (moved from Lesson 15 in 2026)

**Learning intention:** understand how authors create character voice; how Fraillon uses adjectives and verbs to create Subhi's voice.
**Success criteria:** annotate sentences to describe Subhi's voice; emulate Fraillon's writing to create my own character voice.

**Silent starter:** 1) What does it mean "to have a voice"? 2) As a young refugee in detention: complete — "Nobody is listening to me because ___"; "When I try to speak, ___"; "The only thing I can do is ___".

**Definitions:** noun, adjective, verb, affix (prefix, suffix — with examples).

**Core teaching:** p. 7 — '"What am I listening to?" she says, the bored all over her face…' — *bored* (adjective) used as a noun in place of *boredom*. Fraillon swaps word classes by removing the suffix; it makes description vivid and reminds us Subhi is young and English isn't his first language. Annotate in two colours: 1) what's unusual about the word; 2) what it makes you think.

**Practice lines:** "I not hungry much, Subhi love." (p. 3); "…looking at it with that hard on her face…" (p. 15); verbs in place of adjectives: "Tells them about the sun being burning today…" (p. 16). (2025 version also used: "the still and heavy in the tent" (p. 46); "a sort of brave that I haven't felt in ages" (p. 72).)

**Extend:** rewrite in Subhi's voice: "Eli looked really thirsty, and I could tell he was mad."; "The last time the jackets sent a young boy to Alpha, they came back different." Writing task: rewrite your two strongest starter sentences using the technique (model narrative provided). Further: how do the voices of Maá, Subhi and Jimmie differ?

## Lesson 09 — Reading and annotating

**Learning intention:** make notes as you read. **Success:** read to end of chapter 10; three annotation types; at least one open question.

Annotation types: **Notice** (symbols + explanations), **Connections** (own life / world / other texts), **Questions** — closed (single answer), clarifying ("Why does Subhi talk to a rubber duck?"), open ("Does Subhi really think the duck is speaking to him?"; "Why is it okay for children to be placed in adult detention?"). These are the questions *you* have, not questions characters ask.

Flags: blue = notice; orange = connection; purple = question. End-of-lesson check: ≥10 annotations, all types, ≥1 open question.

**Listening along, chapters 6–10:** YouTube audio recordings, read along and annotate.

## CAT 1 — Oral presentation

**Learning intentions:** demonstrate understanding of characters and ideas; write in the voice of a character; present clearly and confidently; practise planning a multi-step task.

Process: read to end of chapter 14 → CAT pre-planning (metacognitive task) → choose one of five presentation options → brainstorm & plan (speech / script / interview templates).

**CAT 1 rubric (When presenting ideas), levels 5–9:**
- **Ideas:** identify parts of the story relating to key ideas → describe characters/events relating to key ideas → describe ideas and issues illustrated through characters and events → explain how different perspectives on ideas are represented → analyse how different people and perspectives are represented.
- **Creating texts:** clear sequence of ideas → develop and organise ideas for a purpose → use ideas/structures/language from examples → appropriate language and structures to raise issues, report events, advance opinions → sustained texts elaborating ideas with deliberate consideration of audience.
- **Responding to literature:** describe some language features used by the author → emulate language features → emulate features that create characterisation → emulate features that influence audience feelings about characters → emulate features that shape audience feelings about key ideas.
- **Presentation skills:** present to a small group → eye contact and clear voice → structured spoken text with appropriate voice/language features → adjust voice and body language to convey ideas in different ways.

**CAT pre-planning sheet:** choose option; up to 8 steps (step 1 = select option; step 8 = present); explanation (goal, why the plan helps, when a plan like this worked before). Self-assessment rubric: Ideas + Critical and Creative Thinking (break task into stages → sequence → justify → evaluate). *(Metacognition capability.)*

**CAT-1 Alternative (modified/EAL):** short presentation about chapter 8; one character; discuss feelings, hopes, how the author shows their voice; 2–3 min (300–450 words); steps include reading a chapter summary and set passages, planning template with sentence starters ("I am talking as…", "S/he feels … because…", "Her/His biggest hope is…", "People should know that…"), cue cards, rehearse, present.

**Student self-assessment (pre-presentation checklist, 10 min):** practice ticks (clear intro, detailed ideas, logical order, connect to context, clear conclusion); two strengths; two improvements; action steps; audience awareness (pace, body language/eye contact, confidence); final reflection.

## Lesson 16 — Writing about how the text is written

**Think-pair-share:** What does the narrator's voice tell us about Subhi? How does the author show life inside the detention centre? Subhi's imagination? Freedom, imprisonment, hope?

**Definitions:** Descriptive writing (senses); Internal dialogue (voice showing what a character thinks — imagined dialogue); Symbolism (repeated objects/motifs — birds = freedom; fence and jackets = imprisonment; camera and photographs = hope).

**Group paragraph (4–5 sentences):** S1 answer the question directly; S2 explain why/how the author writes this way; S3 example with embedded evidence; S4 second example (different part of the story or different aspect); S5 summarise how the two examples prove S1.

## Lesson 17 — Analytic writing

**Word choice:** specific > general (narrative not story); formal > informal (children not kids); words not numerals (seven not 7); family names (Fraillon, not Zana).
**Structure:** introduction/conclusion; an argument (claims → conclusion); chronological sequence; one element at a time; **TEEL** (Topic sentence, Explanation, Evidence/examples, Linking sentence).

**Workshopped model:** "Subhi's imagination is shown by visual descriptions of what Subhi thinks about and the dialogue of the Shakespeare duck. From the beginning of the novel, descriptions of the night sea, or the world stopping when Subhi's head is underwater highlights Subhi's way of escaping from the reality of life in the detention centre."

Evidence to embed: "When I stick my head under the water, the whole world stops. The earth stops spinning, the wind stops blowing, the birds in the trees freeze, and the birds that are flying fall like stones to the ground." (p. 16); duck dialogue: "I look at the duck for a second. I think it gives me a little nod and a wink. 'Well, hello there,' it says." (p. 15); "When I pull out the Shakespeare duck, he looks at me and says, 'She didn't even tell you her name.'" (p. 51)

**Finished model paragraph:** "Subhi's imagination is shown by visual descriptions of what Subhi thinks about and the dialogue of the Shakespeare duck. From the beginning of the novel, descriptions of the night sea, or when the 'earth stops spinning' and 'the birds in the trees freeze' when Subhi's head is underwater highlights Subhi's way of escaping from the reality of life in the detention centre (16). Subhi's imagination is also shown by showing Subhi's Shakespeare duck '[giving him] a little nod and a wink' and speaking to him. The duck's comments, like 'She didn't even tell you her name' (51) allow the reader to know Subhi's innermost thoughts, and the detailed descriptions allow the reader to visualise the ideas in Subhi's head."

## CAT 4 — Writing a text response essay

Essay = extended non-fiction; single explored idea; proper sentences and paragraphs. Text response essay: formal language, audience has read the novel (don't retell); ~5 paragraphs (intro, 3 body, conclusion); must answer a prompt, demonstrate understanding of ideas/events/characters, and explore how the author has written about them.

### CAT 4.1 — Choosing a topic
Prompts (quote/idea + instruction):
1. 'But reading is important.' (p. 39) — Discuss how stories and imagination are important for characters in The Bone Sparrow.
2. 'Soon they'll see that living in here isn't living at all. We just need to show them who we are, that we're people, and then they'll remember.' (p. 108) — Discuss the living conditions for characters inside and outside the centre.
3. The Bone Sparrow explores themes of family and friendship. Discuss.

**Unpacking a prompt** (Edward Scissorhands model): underline content words (distorted vision, suburban life, conformity, comment); build a synonym/antonym word bank; write a reflection on what the prompt asks.

### CAT 4.2 — Developing an argument
An argument = claims leading to a conclusion (validity demonstrated with the lizards/goanna and groobs/perfnicks examples). Elements: contention (start), claims, evidence, conclusion (end).

Sample contentions per prompt (e.g. "In The Bone Sparrow, imagination acts as a distraction that allows characters to survive in a harsh world"; "Zana Fraillon depicts a difference between the way Australians imagine detention and the reality for those living inside"; "The Bone Sparrow shows that friends are a type of family").

Task: write contention; brainstorm claims (Edward Scissorhands model: 6 ideas narrowed to best 3); choose the three claims that best prove the contention (`C4.2w`).

### CAT 4.3 — Writing an introduction
Functions: introduce text and topic; describe the argument; strong first impression. Steps: 1) key-word brainstorm (synonyms/antonyms/related); 2) "big idea" first sentence naming title + author (prepositional phrase + subject + analytic verb — e.g. "In the novel The Bone Sparrow, Zana Fraillon explores the importance of both family and friends for those living in detention"); 3) contention in fresh words; 4) link characters/narrative to prompt by summarising the three claims in one sentence.

### CAT 4.4 — Writing topic sentences
Recap verb/subject/object/prepositional phrase. PowerPoint: nominalisation (two ways); topic sentences. Task: three versions of the topic sentence for claim 1 (subject = character / text / idea), then a topic sentence for each claim.

### CAT 4.5 — Planning the essay
`C4.5w` planner: prompt, contention, introduction (copy in), then per claim — topic sentence, explanation (analytic verbs: highlights, illustrates, demonstrates, suggests, reinforces, indicates, emphasises, reveals, represents, symbolises), evidence (quotes that show how the author created an image/idea, not retelling), link sentence (concluding phrase); conclusion notes.

## CSQ — Claim Support Question strategy

Learning: what a claim is; how to support it with evidence and questions; build a strong body paragraph. Success criteria (Mild/Hot/Spicy): identify a claim / match claim to support / answer simple question with starters → write own claim + support with a quote + question that challenges the claim → discuss what makes a good claim, justify with well-chosen evidence, build a CSQ paragraph outline independently.

Sequence: Would-you-rather claim starter → direct instruction (define C/S/Q; good vs weak claims; model) → matching task (claims ↔ supports; e.g. Claim: Friendship helps characters be brave. Support: Subhi and Jimmie share stories that give them courage.) → pairs choose a scene/symbol, write C + S + Q, combine into an analysis sentence (Claim + Support + So-what), make a 3-panel comic (lywi.com) → exit ticket (choose achieved criterion; prove it with a CSQ).
